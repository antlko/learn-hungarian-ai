This collection is about lexical tendencies of Hungarian nouns on whether they form the possessive with or without *-j* (i.e., *-a/e* as opposed to *-ja/je,* the vowel quality being out of scope here). This is the form (for example *szék* → *széke*) that expresses constructions like *his/her/its chair, …’s chair, the chair of…* This also forms the basis for multiple possessions, such as *his/her/its chairs, …’s chairs, the chairs of…,* as well as the form for multiple possessors *(their chairs).*

Words that end in vowels are simple: they invariably have a linking *-j-* in their third-person possessive forms, e.g. *(alm**a** →) almája, (kört**e** →) körtéje* (these two groups lengthen the final vowel), *hajr**á**ja, portr**é**ja, hobb**i**ja, rögb**i**je, haj**ó**ja, eserny**ő**je, kap**u**ja, gyan**ú**ja, esk**ü**je, gyűr**ű**je*. (Few unadapted foreign common nouns have short *-o* or *-ö* at the end; these are lengthened before this suffix similarly to final *-a/-e,* like *allegrója.)*

Some consonant endings also have a predictable form, namely the one without *-j,* such as words ending in *C CS DZS* and *SZ* *Z S ZS* (sibilant affricates and fricatives) as well as *J*  *NY* *TY* *GY* (palatals): pia**c**a, per**c**e, kul**cs**a, gyümöl**cs**e, bri**dzs**e, bu**sz**a, ré**sz**e, há**z**a, pén**z**e, hú**s**a, ver**s**e, rú**zs**a, ri**zs**e, ha**j**a, fe**j**e, sú**ly**a, he**ly**e, lá**ny**a, fé**ny**e, pon**ty**a, pin**ty**e, á**gy**a, he**gy**e.

In the table below, the suffixed forms are given, but they are linked to the main lemma entry. The left column collects forms without *-j;* the right column, those with *-j.* Under each consonant header, the first row is for words with a short final vowel (before the consonant); the second, for words with a long final vowel. The third row is shared between irregular words (mostly in the left column) and words with a final consonant cluster (mostly in the right column). The fourth row (if it exists), merged between the columns, shows words whose possessive forms differ, depending on meaning.

Though there are clear tendencies, possessive suffixes need to be learned with each word. In certain groups of cases, individual native speakers may disagree with each other about the preferable version or even use a form inconsistently themselves. Different generations may prefer one or another variant (e.g. sivatag(j)a, usually moving towards *-j* in time); usage in archaism may similarly differ from current usage (e.g. archaic családai instead of present-day családjai). Geographical, dialectal differences exist as well (e.g. tányéra as opposed to standard tányérja). Different compounds with the same noun at the end might take the two form variants in a different distribution.

As a last resort, one may be advised to use the *-ja/je* forms (as opposed to *-j*-less forms), as it is less likely to interfere with comprehension. Erring on the side of the *-j* form often sounds less "outlandish" than the opposite, because the scope of words forming the possessive without *-j* tends to be a closed, improductive group (except certain analogies) and there is a (slow) expansion of the *-j* forms among native speakers. (Also, this type of mistake is more common with children learning to speak, e.g. *szemüvegje instead of standard szemüvege.)

The *-j-*less forms are on the left, while forms with *-j-,* on the right. The order of words presented within the cell of a given final consonant is by ending type or lexical grouping, and within the vowel-based paragraphs, short words first, then longer ones. Most cases of alternation (where existing sources contradict, typically where native speakers are inconsistent) have been usually grouped into either of the two columns (with or without *-j-*), noting this fact with a *(j)* in parentheses.

The list is not exhaustive: many uncommon and domain-specific words have been omitted. The abundance of examples is intended to help readers deduce and discover regularities for themselves.
